01FAILURE MODEL

A failed motor and a bad measurement are different problems

The motor fault model can reduce available torque or remove it completely. Faults can persist or appear intermittently. A wheel can keep rotating after motor torque disappears, so nonzero wheel speed alone is not proof that the motor works.

A tachometer problem affects the reported wheel speed rather than the physical motor. Missing samples, stale values or measurement errors can make a healthy motor appear unresponsive. The monitor therefore needs both timing checks and a model of the expected response.

During training, injected faults create experience. Their configured frequency is a training choice, not an estimate of real spacecraft failure rates.

02DETECTION / ISOLATION

Several checks, each with its own job

A watchdog checks whether fresh tachometer data arrive. A motor monitor asks whether the measured change in wheel speed agrees with the predicted motor torque. Hysteresis and persistence turn this evidence into health states without reacting to every small fluctuation.

The motor monitor estimates authority over a window of usable samples. Let x be predicted torque and y be the torque inferred from wheel acceleration, with the modeled friction term included. A fit through the origin gives:

estimated authority = Σ(x · y) / Σ(x²)

The implementation bounds the estimate between zero and one and requires enough excitation. Using a window avoids dividing by a small command from one noisy sample. It is a regression estimate, not an exponential moving average, even though an internal field retains the old wheel_authority_ewma name.

Sample timestamps are used to calculate the change in wheel speed. The fit still pairs that response with the current motor-torque prediction, rather than a stored average over the matching sample interval. With changing commands or latency, these can refer to different moments. A window can soften the effect, but does not align them automatically.

03RECOVERY / SUPERVISION

Detection is not yet a recovery decision

Fault monitoring supplies health information. The supervisor uses it, together with estimation and mission conditions, to select behavior. This is the connection between FDIR and modes such as DEGRADED_POINTING or SAFE_SUN: a health problem can change what the spacecraft should attempt.

Hysteresis uses different evidence for declaring a problem and accepting recovery. Persistence prevents one good or bad sample from repeatedly switching the state.

There is also a practical difficulty after declaring a motor failed. In motor_direct, its commands are suppressed. This protects the command path, but it can remove the excitation needed to assess the motor again. If the simulated fault disappears, recovery still needs suitable new evidence or a deliberate reassessment mechanism.

04CONTROL AUTHORITY

Similar symptoms can have different causes

Separate motor and tachometer health states help organize the evidence. They do not make every fault uniquely identifiable. A useful question is: what else could produce this measurement?

Observed symptom Possible explanations
Wheel speed hardly changes after a command Lost motor authority; a small or limited command; motor lag; a stale tachometer value.
Estimated authority drops A real torque loss; noisy speed differences; model error; command and sample timing mismatch.
No new wheel-speed data Sensor or delivery failure; a timing problem; a missed sample. It does not by itself prove a motor failure.
Pointing becomes worse Reduced wheel authority; a poor state estimate; saturation; a reference the remaining actuators cannot follow.

The fourth wheel gives redundancy, but available control still depends on the remaining wheel geometry, torque limits and speeds. The useful outcome is continued control within the authority that remains, not simply a “healthy” label.

05EVIDENCE TO BUILD

Measure detection and control together

The next fault management update should use matched seeds and fault schedules for learned and conventional controllers. Evaluation needs nominal periods as well as injected faults; otherwise false alarms remain invisible.

  • Detection: time to actionable diagnosis, missed faults, false alarms, and sensitivity to low excitation.
  • Isolation: whether the affected wheel is identified and whether healthy wheels are unnecessarily de-rated.
  • Recovery: time to restored authority belief, number of mode transitions, and behavior during repeated interruptions.
  • Mission impact: peak pointing error, time outside tolerance, saturation, and time to reacquire the target.

Aggregate fault-management results and validated switching thresholds remain to be published. The current contribution is an architecture in which hidden faults, estimated authority, and supervisory decisions can be examined separately.
