01FAILURE MODEL

A failed motor vs. a vanished wheel

The project’s fault-tolerance problem begins with four reaction wheels and intermittent loss of motor authority. An injected motor-off fault removes drive torque; it does not mean the rotor disappears or instantly stops spinning. Stored momentum and passive mechanical effects still matter.

The supplied B3 scenario randomly selects a wheel and includes failure and recovery events. Its configured failure and recovery hazards are 0.1 s⁻¹ and 0.5 s⁻¹, with zero retained drive torque during failure. These are accelerated training-scenario settings, not spacecraft component reliability estimates.

Three distinct events must remain separate: the simulated actuator loses authority, telemetry supports a diagnosis, and the supervisor changes its decision. Giving the controller the injected fault flag would erase the uncertainty this project is intended to model.

02DETECTION / ISOLATION

Infer authority from a response, not a label

The fault-management path uses delivered telemetry to estimate how much wheel authority remains. Current implementation moves the authority estimate to a windowed regression approach, the supplied configuration uses a 0.4-second window and a minimum of 12 samples. The health estimate is a belief about actuator response, distinct from the simulator’s true state.

The detection problem is harder than checking whether a wheel turns. Low commanded torque may provide little excitation; friction and dead zones can suppress motion; delivery delay can make a healthy response appear late. A wheel may also continue rotating after losing motor torque. Diagnosis must relate commands to observed response with these effects in mind.

A continuous authority estimate can inform the controller without implying that one noisy estimate should immediately trigger a mode transition. Diagnostic confidence, permission to use an actuator, and permission to resume the mission are different decisions.

03RECOVERY / SUPERVISION

Recovery needs stronger evidence

A simulated recovery event restores physical motor capability. It does not instantly establish that the onboard estimate has recovered or that the mission can safely resume. The supervisor retains authority over operating modes and target overrides, rather than delegating those decisions to the learned policy.

Hysteresis and persistence

For a recovery policy, separate entry and exit conditions create hysteresis: the evidence required to declare recovery differs from the evidence that triggered degradation. Persistence or dwell requirements add a time condition. Together, these can prevent noisy measurements from repeatedly toggling modes, while introducing a deliberate delay that needs evaluation.

Implementation boundary: telemetry-derived authority and supervisory overrides are part of the documented project. Exact fault/recovery thresholds, hysteresis bands, and dwell settings are not established by the currently available source record. They are a release-audit item here, not a claimed validated recovery mechanism.

04CONTROL AUTHORITY

Redundancy is a feasible-torque question

Four reaction wheels offer more actuator channels than the three body-torque dimensions. If the surviving three wheel axes remain linearly independent, a body-torque solution may still exist after one loss. That geometric fact does not guarantee enough torque, momentum capacity, or acceptable pointing performance.

Motor limits and wheel-speed limits bound the reachable torque set. A remaining wheel near saturation can make an otherwise full-rank arrangement operationally inadequate. Magnetic torquers provide a separate momentum-management path, with authority constrained by the local magnetic-field direction.

In current implementatiion an actor receives four estimated wheel-authority values along with tracking errors, wheel speeds, and previous actions. It can condition its commands on degraded authority instead of always acting as though every motor is healthy. An agent was deliberately traind to control with a randomly interrupted wheel to learn torque redistribution while one wheel suddenly lost.

However, the policy highly depends on the quality of the health estimate and on the failures conditions represented during training. A classical fault-aware allocator is an essential comparator to ensure an overal agent fault tolerance.

05EVIDENCE TO BUILD

Measure detection and control together

The next fault management update should use matched seeds and fault schedules for learned and conventional controllers. Evaluation needs nominal periods as well as injected faults; otherwise false alarms remain invisible.

  • Detection: time to actionable diagnosis, missed faults, false alarms, and sensitivity to low excitation.
  • Isolation: whether the affected wheel is identified and whether healthy wheels are unnecessarily de-rated.
  • Recovery: time to restored authority belief, number of mode transitions, and behavior during repeated interruptions.
  • Mission impact: peak pointing error, time outside tolerance, saturation, and time to reacquire the target.

Aggregate fault-management results and validated switching thresholds remain to be published. The current contribution is an architecture in which hidden faults, estimated authority, and supervisory decisions can be examined separately.
